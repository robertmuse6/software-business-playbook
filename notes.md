# From first use to a business

Editorial notes · 27 October 2026

## Read first

A tiny useful offer is the beginning of a customer journey, not proof of a scalable business. Work through free → passive → active → paid → recurring core. Define the event at each gate, then test distribution, pricing and delivery around that event. Audience, product, price and activation are still hypotheses.

## Evidence limits

The supplied videos are not presented as complete audio-verified transcripts. The SaaS clip has title metadata only. Partial sales retrieval does not establish full four-hour coverage. Frameworks below distinguish source teaching from original applications. No private interview details or personal business figures are reproduced.

## Recommended next decisions, ranked

1. Choose a buyer situation and one narrow job. A large platform loses for now because it combines too many untested jobs.

2. Test a bounded free output and one permitted distribution channel. Unlimited free service loses until its cost and role are clear.

3. Define and observe a first-value event. Counting signups as success loses because it says nothing about usefulness.

4. Test a paid boundary and delivery economics. A recurring subscription loses if the customer job does not recur.

5. Observe repeat value before adding sellers or traffic. More acquisition loses when delivery is unreliable.

## Lifecycle cards

### 1. Give away one small result, not a miniature company.

Stage: Free offer

The free thing should finish one useful job and point naturally toward the larger problem you could solve.

One urgent job → A tiny useful output → A next step

Why: A broad life-coaching promise is hard to try and harder to judge. A narrow output makes the value visible before asking for trust.

Decision rule: If people collect it but never use it, shrink the job or improve the fit before adding features.

Source example/evidence: Paul Graham describes Stripe founders setting up users directly and Airbnb founders visiting hosts. Early manual work can uncover what must later become software. The official $100M Leads resource page lists lead magnets and first-client materials; the landing-page titles alone do not establish a detailed lesson.

Action: Pick one buyer situation and write the output someone can finish in one sitting. Test a manual version before software.

Measure: Track useful outputs completed, not downloads alone. Record what people do next.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: a short decision worksheet that ends in one real-world test could lead to a supported decision sprint. This is one option, not the chosen product.

Sources: PG, HL

### 2. Choose a channel where the problem already appears.

Stage: Free offer

Distribution starts with a specific person in a specific situation, not with a list of every marketing channel.

Trigger → Place they seek help → Useful invitation

Why: A channel that produces attention from the wrong audience makes the later funnel look broken.

Decision rule: If a channel gets clicks but no useful completions, inspect buyer fit and promise before buying more traffic.

Source example/evidence: Stripe separates low-touch software sold through product/marketing from high-touch software supported by human selling. The channel and price have to pay for the chosen motion. Hormozi’s branding talk adds a useful distinction: reach makes people aware; repeated pairings shape what they associate with you. He uses Nike and athletes as an illustration.

Action: Compare a warm introduction, a relevant community and useful public content. Choose one for a small first test. Respect community rules and consent.

Measure: For each channel: qualified visits → starts → useful completions → conversations. Keep the counts separate.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: reach people near a real decision deadline through an existing trusted group. Do not assume followers or a mailing list already exist.

Sources: SB, PG, H2

### 3. Make free a bounded business decision.

Stage: Free offer

A free worksheet, a time-limited trial and a permanent free product are different offers with different costs.

Free resource → Trial of paid value → Ongoing free tier

Why: Unlimited free coaching can attract demand you cannot afford to serve. Free must have a job in the business.

Decision rule: If free value is expensive and rarely leads to a useful next step, cap usage or test a trial or paid pilot instead.

Source example/evidence: Stripe pricing guidance distinguishes the underlying product from its packaging and warns against unlimited plans. That is guidance, not a rule that freemium always loses.

Action: Choose what is free, for whom, for how long, the usage cap and the path to paid. State the boundary before signup.

Measure: Cost per useful free completion; support minutes; proportion reaching the next value event.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: offer one self-serve diagnostic free; test human support as a paid pilot. No price is selected.

Sources: SP, HL

### 4. Decide what would disprove the offer before you launch it.

Stage: Free offer

A free offer tests usefulness and distribution. It does not prove willingness to pay.

Prediction → Observed action → Keep / change / stop

Why: People can praise a free tool without having a problem worth paying to solve.

Decision rule: If the promised output does not help anyone act, change the job before polishing the landing page.

Source example/evidence: The first-users examples in Paul Graham are about direct learning and service. They do not establish a universal conversion benchmark.

Action: Write one falsifiable prediction, recruit a small relevant group and observe use. Agree a decision date rather than collecting feedback forever.

Measure: Use raw counts with denominators and interview notes. Small samples are directional, not statistical certainty.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: watch whether a user takes the next agreed action after a decision worksheet, not whether they say the worksheet is impressive.

Sources: PG

### 5. Define passive so you can tell who is actually stuck.

Stage: Passive user

A follower, an email subscriber, a signup and an unused free account are different states. Choose your own event definition.

Reached → Opted in → Not yet helped

Why: Without definitions, growth can be a pile of signups while nobody gets a result.

Decision rule: If you cannot identify the transition event, instrument it before claiming the funnel improved.

Source example/evidence: Amplitude treats activation as discovering and experiencing product value, which is different from a registration event.

Action: Write passive = a specific consented entry event, with no first-value event yet. Separate anonymous visitors from identified accounts.

Measure: Passive users by channel and age since entry. Do not mix new arrivals with old inactive accounts.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: passive means someone saved a decision tool but has not completed a useful next-action plan. This needs testing for the actual product.

Sources: AC

### 6. The invitation should promise the next result, not more content.

Stage: Passive user

A useful prompt tells someone what they will finish and how much work it takes.

Current situation → One small task → Visible payoff

Why: An account welcome email is not a reason to return. The user needs a reason tied to their own job.

Decision rule: If people start but stall, remove work or explain the payoff. If they never start, revisit the promise and audience.

Source example/evidence: Stripe pricing examples stress continuing the sales message and reducing decision fatigue. We apply that principle here to onboarding, not as a measured result.

Action: Show one concrete next step, a worked example and a clear way to start. Ask permission for reminders; do not manufacture urgency.

Measure: Entry → first task started; started → completed; where people stop.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: ask a person to choose one decision they need to make, then show what a finished action plan looks like.

Sources: SP

### 7. A human-assisted first run can reveal the software you need.

Stage: Passive user

Do the work alongside early users before automating the wrong sequence.

Watch → Help → Record friction → Build the repeat

Why: Automation saves the most time when the workflow is known. Until then it can hide why a user struggles.

Decision rule: If every user needs a different service, narrow the job before writing a generalized platform.

Source example/evidence: Paul Graham describes founders manually recruiting and delighting early users, including the Collisons doing installation themselves.

Action: Observe several first runs. Note questions, manual interventions, missing inputs and unsafe outputs. Build only the repeated bottleneck.

Measure: Minutes to first useful result; interventions per user; repeated friction points.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: manually help a person complete one decision sprint. Separate what required human judgment from what a form or workflow can do.

Sources: PG

### 8. Measure the whole handoff, including silence.

Stage: Passive user

A signup can fail because of fit, effort, confusion, timing or missing trust. These need different fixes.

Right person? → Clear next step? → Worth the effort? → Enough trust?

Why: More reminders will not fix a product that asks for too much sensitive information before delivering value.

Decision rule: If trust is the blocker, show limits, privacy and examples. If effort is the blocker, shorten the path.

Source example/evidence: This is an original diagnostic built from activation guidance and early-user observation principles, not a Hormozi quotation.

Action: Ask a few non-starters what happened. Review the actual first-run experience before inventing an engagement campaign.

Measure: Time to first value, abandonment step, and reasons for not continuing.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: let users try a non-sensitive example before asking for a personal life history.

Sources: AC, PG

### 9. Activation means a useful action happened.

Stage: Active user

Define a first-value event tied to the job. Merely opening the app or chatting is not enough.

Input → Useful output → Real action

Why: If the core job is making progress on a decision, the product must connect advice to an action outside the chat.

Decision rule: If activation does not predict later value, change the definition. A correlation is a clue, not proof that forcing the event causes retention.

Source example/evidence: Amplitude Compass investigates actions associated with later retention. It helps find candidate aha moments; it does not establish causation by itself.

Action: Draft one activation event and verify it with observed users. Then check whether activated users return more often than others.

Measure: Activated users / eligible new users, within an explicitly named window. Compare by cohort and channel.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: activation could be a completed action plan plus one attempted real-world step. The right event is still unknown.

Sources: AM

### 10. Shorten the path to value before adding a feature.

Stage: Active user

The smallest helpful product is often a complete narrow workflow rather than a shallow version of a huge platform.

Arrive → Do the job → See result

Why: Every extra question, screen or permission is work before the user knows the product helps.

Decision rule: If speed increases but output quality drops, restore the step or redesign it. Fast bad advice is not activation.

Source example/evidence: Original application of the activation and first-user sources. No source establishes a particular feature roadmap for this venture.

Action: Remove one step between arrival and the useful event. Keep safety and necessary consent. Compare the next cohort, not just time-on-site.

Measure: Median time to first value; completion count; quality of the output.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: start with one decision route, not a full catalogue of coaching, learning and opportunities.

Sources: AC, PG

### 11. Separate motivation from a product that reliably works.

Stage: Active user

Software adds a quality obligation: the same promise must work when the founder is not there.

Useful → Reliable → Understandable → Safe

Why: A compelling pitch can sell a first session. It cannot repair lost work, misleading recommendations or broken access.

Decision rule: If the product regularly needs rescue, stop adding acquisition until the repeated failure is fixed.

Source example/evidence: Stripe describes SaaS as an ongoing delivery relationship. The specific safety checklist is our proposed operating practice.

Action: List the failures that would destroy trust. Add basic error handling, saved work, correction routes and clear product limits before scaling.

Measure: Task success; data loss incidents; support response time; human escalation rate.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: a coaching tool should say when it lacks context and make it easy to correct an inaccurate recommendation. It is not a substitute for professional care.

Sources: SB

### 12. A good free experience can still be the wrong paid business.

Stage: Active user

Test whether people want a continuing tool, a one-off outcome or human help. Those are different businesses.

One-off job → Recurring workflow → Assisted service

Why: A deadline-driven decision may end. Forcing a subscription onto an occasional job can create avoidable churn.

Decision rule: If the need ends after one outcome, test a one-off model instead of assuming recurring software revenue.

Source example/evidence: Stripe distinguishes low-touch and high-touch models and discusses pricing relative to value and sales cost.

Action: Offer clear alternatives in customer conversations: one-off sprint, recurring tool, assisted service. Ask for a real purchase decision only when you can deliver.

Measure: Reasons for choosing a model; completed paid pilots; delivery cost. Interest is not a sale.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: a decision sprint could be one-off while an ongoing team workflow could justify recurring use. Neither is chosen yet.

Sources: SB, SP

### 13. Charge for a clear outcome or capability, not a vague upgrade.

Stage: Paid user

The paid boundary should be explainable in one sentence. More buttons are not automatically more value.

Free result → Paid job → Clear boundary

Why: A customer needs to know why the paid version changes what they can accomplish.

Decision rule: If users hit the boundary but do not buy, inspect value, fit, trust and price separately. Do not assume the price alone is wrong.

Source example/evidence: In Stripe’s Cirtru example, the key packaging distinction is a tool versus a more managed outcome. It is a pricing critique, not proof of a revenue lift.

Action: Write: free helps you do X; paid helps you do Y. State scope, duration, support and cancellation plainly.

Measure: Paywall exposure → checkout start → successful payment → delivered result.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: paid might add a supported follow-through sprint to a free decision map. Do not promise career or income outcomes you cannot control.

Sources: SP

### 14. Price must support the way you sell and deliver.

Stage: Paid user

Self-serve software, sales-assisted software and human coaching have different costs.

Price → Variable cost → Contribution → Acquisition cost

Why: A low monthly price with long sales calls and unlimited support may be a service business losing money in software clothing.

Decision rule: If each sale adds more delivery cost than revenue, fix scope, price or delivery before adding sellers.

Source example/evidence: Stripe’s business-of-SaaS guide explains why pricing and low-/high-touch selling must fit together.

Action: Estimate payment fees, AI/API usage, human delivery and support per customer. Use contribution after those costs to set an acquisition budget.

Measure: Contribution per customer; cost to acquire a paying customer; payback period using the same time unit.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: price a pilot from actual delivery effort and buyer value. Use the calculator to test assumptions, not as a recommended price.

Sources: SB, SP

### 15. Use discovery to test fit before you try to close.

Stage: Paid user

Find the real situation, previous attempts, deadline and decision process. A no-fit customer is a useful discovery outcome.

Situation → Impact → Tried before → Next step

Why: Scripts help only when they surface a real buying problem. Pressure can make a sale that later turns into a refund and lost trust.

Decision rule: If there is no urgent job or credible way to help, say so. Do not invent scarcity or shame people into paying.

Source example/evidence: In the sales compilation, the transcript-derived 4:42–4:58 section puts the prospect ahead of the sale. In the team video he emphasizes prospect knowledge, not only product knowledge. The questions here are an adaptation, not a direct quote.

Action: Ask: What are you trying to get done? Why now? What have you tried? What would a useful result look like? Who decides? Then reflect it back.

Measure: Qualified conversations; fit/no-fit reasons; agreed next steps; refunds and downstream use.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: demo the workflow using a buyer’s agreed non-sensitive example, then ask whether the output solves the stated job.

Sources: SB, H3, HT

### 16. Treat objections as missing information before treating them as resistance.

Stage: Paid user

Too expensive, no time and need to think can mean several things. Diagnose rather than recite a comeback.

Concern → Clarifying question → Evidence → Decision

Why: A buyer who does not understand the commitment needs clarity. A buyer who cannot afford it needs a safe no, not financing pressure.

Decision rule: If the concern is real product weakness, fix it. If it is poor fit, disqualify.

Source example/evidence: At transcript-derived 25:01–25:30 in the sales compilation, he groups concerns around time, money, fit, authority/partner and avoidance. Some later high-ticket reframing is aggressive; we retain diagnosis and an honest no, not pressure.

Action: Ask what specifically makes the purchase hard. Show relevant evidence or limits. Offer a smaller appropriate scope only if it still helps.

Measure: Objection categories; resolution; later activation and refunds, not close rate alone.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: if someone asks how this differs from a general chatbot, show the concrete workflow, continuity and evidence rather than making broad AI claims.

Sources: SB, H3

### 17. Recurring revenue must follow recurring value.

Stage: Recurring core

Payment alone does not tell you the product remains useful. A core user repeatedly completes the important job.

New need → Useful action → Visible progress → Return

Why: Habitual app opening is not the goal when the user’s job happens weekly, monthly or only near a decision.

Decision rule: If the job is completed and the user leaves satisfied, distinguish success from product failure. Consider one-off pricing or a new adjacent job.

Source example/evidence: Stripe treats retention and churn as central to a subscription business. Our cadence rule applies that to an episodic coaching context.

Action: Define the natural cadence of the job and the event that demonstrates repeat value. Measure retention at that cadence.

Measure: Cohort retention for the core value event; paid renewal; cancellations; outcomes achieved.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: a recurring tool may support weekly follow-through. A decision sprint may legitimately end. Product research must choose.

Sources: SB

### 18. Cohorts tell you whether the product improves.

Stage: Recurring core

Compare people who started together. A growing total can hide weak retention in every group.

Start cohort → First value → Repeat value → Renewal

Why: New acquisition can replace churned users and make a weak product look healthy.

Decision rule: If later cohorts improve, investigate what changed. If they worsen after broader acquisition, inspect fit.

Source example/evidence: Amplitude’s analytics guidance and Stripe’s SaaS model support separating acquisition, activation and retention. No benchmark is asserted here.

Action: Group users by start period and channel. At a fixed age, count who repeated the core event and who renewed. Show raw denominators.

Measure: Week-N value-event retention = returning cohort users / eligible cohort users. Do not mix calendar totals with cohort rates.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: track a chosen weekly action event only if weekly use matches the actual customer job.

Sources: AC, SB

### 19. Solve why people leave before building a win-back campaign.

Stage: Recurring core

A reminder cannot fix a finished job, bad advice, a broken product or a price mismatch.

Finished? → Failed? → Forgot? → Wrong fit?

Why: Each churn reason implies a different action. One retention tactic for all reasons wastes work.

Decision rule: If failure is involuntary payment loss, improve billing recovery. If the job is complete, change model or accept the exit.

Source example/evidence: The reason-based decision tree is original applied guidance. Stripe supplies the underlying subscription model, not these precise categories.

Action: Read a sample of cancellation and inactivity cases. Separate completed need, missing value, friction, trust, price and failed payment.

Measure: Reason counts; voluntary versus payment-related churn; recovered value, not email opens.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: do not shame a coaching user for leaving. Make cancellation clear, preserve their work and offer re-entry when the job returns.

Sources: SB

### 20. Scale the narrow loop that already works.

Stage: Recurring core

Repeat the working buyer → value → payment → return loop before adding channels, features and people together.

Proven segment → Repeatable workflow → Affordable delivery → More volume

Why: Changing everything at once makes it impossible to know what produced the result.

Decision rule: If growth breaks quality or economics, reduce the load and fix the limiting rail before spending more.

Source example/evidence: Paul Graham supports learning manually early; Stripe supplies the economic constraints. Neither guarantees speed or scale.

Action: Increase one dimension at a time and watch quality, support and contribution. Add a person only to a named recurring bottleneck.

Measure: Channel-level paid cohorts; contribution; support burden; delivery quality.

Application hypothesis: Hypothesis: keep a small core workflow stable while testing one new acquisition channel. This is a proposed experiment, not a growth promise.

Sources: PG, SB

## Business rails

### A software business has several rails, not one sales funnel.

The same customer promise has to survive every handoff.

- Distribution | Reach the right situation | Which channel brings people with this job now? | Qualified arrivals by source

- Product value | Finish the important job | What useful event tells us it worked? | Task success + time to value

- Retention | Deliver value again | Does this job actually recur? At what cadence? | Value-event cohort retention

- Economics | Afford the promise | Does price cover delivery, AI usage and support? | Contribution + acquisition payback

- Trust and operations | Keep the promise safely | Can people correct, leave and recover their work? | Errors + support + cancellations

- Team and learning | Fix the limiting step | What bottleneck needs a person or a system next? | Throughput + quality of that step

### Choose the selling motion after you understand the job.

None of these is automatically the mature version of another.

- Self-serve tool | Low sales effort per buyer | Fits a job users can try and understand themselves. | Loses when setup, risk or judgment needs human help.

- Sales-assisted software | Human help to reach a useful deployment | Fits a valuable, complicated purchase where price can fund sales. | Loses when every small purchase needs a long call.

- Coaching-enabled service | People deliver important parts of the outcome | Fits early learning or jobs needing real human support. | Loses when marketed as scalable software while delivery stays bespoke.

- One-off sprint | A bounded outcome with a finish line | Fits a decision or project that legitimately ends. | Loses when ongoing value and frequent repeat use are actually the job.

### Build the smallest reliable loop before a platform.

Proposed operating checklist, not a universal technology stack.

- Access and consent | Only collect what this job needs | Explain use of sensitive information and who can see it. | Do not add contact scraping or surprise social accountability.

- Core workflow | Input → useful output → action | Save progress; handle incomplete inputs and corrections. | Do not confuse a chat response with a completed customer job.

- Measurement | Name events and denominators | Track source, first useful event, repeat event and payment status. | Avoid logging private conversations as analytics payloads.

- Billing | Clear price, scope and exit | Handle receipts, failed payments, cancellation and deletion requests. | Do not begin with hidden renewals or unsupported outcome guarantees.

- Reliability and support | Recover when something goes wrong | Test broken states; keep a human escalation path. | No need for elaborate infrastructure before a small pilot.

- AI quality, if used | Evaluate usefulness and failure | Test representative cases, uncertain advice, harmful outputs and cost caps. | A stronger model does not by itself establish coaching quality.

## Sales and team

### A first seller needs a system that the founder can already teach.

Hiring a closer is not a substitute for discovering the buyer or fixing activation.

Repeatable buyer → Honest offer → Successful use → Teachably won sales

Source prescription: Hormozi says that if the founder has no good calls to teach from, the founder should first sell successfully and record them.

Applied action: Before hiring, assemble a buyer profile, fit/no-fit rules, sample calls obtained with consent, a demo, evidence of useful outcomes and a stable source of qualified conversations.

Rule: If users pay but fail to reach value, product/delivery is the bottleneck. If qualified demand exceeds founder capacity and outcomes hold, a sales hire may help.

Boundary: Readiness is an editorial gate, not a universal number of customers.

Sources: HT, SB

### Test coachability in a live role-play.

A confident interview performance does not show whether someone can improve.

Role-play → One clear correction → Repeat → Observe change

Source prescription: Hormozi tests responsiveness, listening and coachability by giving feedback and repeating a role-play.

Applied action: Give a candidate the same buyer scenario, offer and honest constraints. Assess listening, problem diagnosis, accurate promises and response to feedback.

Rule: If they ignore a correction or make unsupported claims to win, do not mistake confidence for ability.

Boundary: Use a consistent fair rubric. This site does not prescribe employment terms or unpaid work.

Sources: HT

### Teach the customer’s situation before the script.

The best product tour still fails when it solves the wrong problem.

Why here? → What hurts? → What tried? → Permission to show

Source prescription: The team video uses a question-based sequence: clarify, label the problem, review past experience, ask permission to explain, address stated problems, ask for a decision, discuss concerns, reinforce.

Applied action: Write a conversation map, not a monologue. Demo only the workflow that answers the stated job. Ask the buyer to perform the key action.

Rule: If the rep cannot explain who should not buy, the training is incomplete.

Boundary: No polished acronym expansion is presented as verbatim because the retrieved automated wording is noisy.

Sources: HT, H3

### Increase live calls after demonstrated readiness.

Training is a progression, not a deck sent on day one.

Listen → Practice → Pass → Limited calls → Full load

Source prescription: Hormozi prescribes about 14 days, around 40 example calls and a half schedule after passing role-play. These are his operating numbers, not requirements.

Applied action: Adapt the progression to your volume and product complexity. Use consented calls or synthetic scenarios, a quality rubric and supervised first calls.

Rule: If lead quality or call quality collapses, do not solve it by giving an unready rep more leads.

Boundary: Readiness includes correct promises, fit diagnosis and the handoff to activation.

Sources: HT

### Coach one observable behavior, then check whether it changed.

Feedback needs a practice loop. “Be more confident” is not a drill.

Listen → Pick one behavior → Practice → Live call → Check again

Source prescription: He describes listening to one or two calls, choosing the highest-impact changes and drilling them through role-play, with dated notes across weeks.

Applied action: Example: last call the rep demoed before learning the job. Practice asking and reflecting the buyer’s problem first. Review the next call for that behavior.

Rule: If feedback repeats without a changed behavior, simplify the drill or identify the missing skill.

Boundary: His example allocation is30 minutes review,5 feedback,25 practice. Treat it as a sample, not a staffing formula.

Sources: HT

### Reward customers who stay helped, not only money collected.

Upfront cash can look good while overselling creates churn and refunds.

Qualified fit → Collected payment → Activated use → Retained value

Source prescription: The source discusses cash-up-front optimization, leaderboards and performance rules. Those are context-specific prescriptions.

Applied action: Pair sales metrics with activated paying customers, refunds, retention, accurate promises and support burden. Review poor-fit deals, not just losses.

Rule: If close rate rises while activation or retention falls, inspect incentives and the promises made.

Boundary: Do not import forced ranking, firing quotas or financial pressure tactics into a tiny team.

Sources: HT, SB

### The best salesperson is not automatically the best manager.

Management requires patient diagnosis and practice, not just personal closing ability.

Observe → Teach → Motivate → Maintain standards

Source prescription: Hormozi describes a good manager as stable, giving, able to lead by example, motivate and train. He describes about an hour per rep weekly after onboarding, more early.

Applied action: Protect actual coaching capacity. Keep dated behavior notes and a shared definition of a good call and customer fit.

Rule: If nobody has time to listen and coach, adding reps may multiply mistakes.

Boundary: Manager bandwidth is a capacity constraint, not an optional meeting. His time estimate needs local testing.

Sources: HT

### Build the team around the bottleneck, not a standard org chart.

Early software roles can be combined, but the responsibilities cannot be ignored.

Find job → Build loop → Deliver safely → Learn → Grow

Source prescription: The sales-team source explains sales training. It does not establish a software engineering hiring sequence.

Applied action: Assign named ownership for customer learning, product/engineering, measurement, delivery/support and distribution. Choose the next hire from observed repeated work or failures.

Rule: If the workflow is still bespoke, an engineer may automate the wrong service. If the workflow is stable but unreliable, a marketer will amplify failure.

Boundary: This is a proposed early-team map, not a claim that a fixed five-person team is required.

Sources: HT, AC, SB

## Complete illustrative coaching workflow

Invented example, not a case study or selected roadmap: a person comparing two career moves uses a ten-minute sheet to list options, constraints and a reversible test. A passive user saves it without finishing a useful comparison. An active user completes it and attempts a real-world test. A paid supported sprint could add feedback and a review, with a finish date and no job/income guarantee. A recurring workflow is appropriate only if the useful action cycle recurs. If the decision is complete, a one-off model may fit better. Test distribution through one relevant permitted channel; price only when a deliverable and its cost are clear.

Illustrative offer: "The free sheet helps you compare two options and choose a test. The supported sprint adds feedback on your plan and a review after you try it. It does not guarantee a job, an admission or a salary increase. Would that support be useful for the decision you described?"

If the buyer says a general chatbot already does the job, test that objection honestly. A workflow and follow-through must provide observable value; a new interface alone is not a defensible difference. Accept a no.
